    a: >-

    a: >-
      In 2NF, and no non-key attribute depends on another non-key attribute.
  - q: >-
      What is a transitive dependency?
    a: >-
      When A determines B and B determines C, so C depends on A only through B. For example StudentID determines TutorID, which determines TutorRoom.

      Summarise 1NF, 2NF and 3NF in one sentence.
    a: >-
      Every non-key attribute must depend on **the key, the whole key, and nothing but the key**.
  - q: >-
      What is a functional dependency?
    a: >-
      When one attribute's value determines another's. Written $A \rightarrow B$: knowing $A$ fixes $B$.

      Give two benefits of normalisation.
    a: >-
      Each fact is stored once, so updates cannot leave inconsistent copies; and less space is used because nothing is duplicated.

Normalisation is a procedure. Given a badly organised table, following the steps
produces a well-organised set of tables, without requiring insight about the
particular data. That is what makes it examinable and what makes it useful.

The whole thing rests on one idea.

## Functional dependency

$A \rightarrow B$ means: if you know $A$, then $B$ is determined. Knowing a
StudentID tells you the student's name. Knowing an ISBN tells you the title.

Normalisation asks, of every attribute: **what determines this?** If the answer is
anything other than the primary key of the table it is sitting in, it is in the
wrong table.

## First normal form

Every attribute holds one **atomic** value, and there are no repeating groups.

Violations look like this:

| StudentID | Name | Subjects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| S1 | Turing | Maths, CS, Physics |

or this:

| StudentID | Name | Subject1 | Subject2 | Subject3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| S1 | Turing | Maths | CS | Physics |

The first cannot be searched — finding everyone taking CS means picking apart
strings. The second caps subjects at three, wastes columns on students taking one,
and makes "how many students take CS?" require checking three columns.

The fix is a separate row per value, in a table of its own:

| StudentID | SubjectCode |
| --- | --- |
| S1 | MATH |
| S1 | COMP |
| S1 | PHYS |

## Second normal form

In 1NF, and **no partial dependencies** — every non-key attribute depends on the
whole primary key.

This can only be violated when the key is composite, so if your key is a single
attribute, 2NF is automatic.

Take an order line table keyed on (OrderID, ProductID):

| OrderID | ProductID | Quantity | ProductName | ProductPrice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1001 | P7 | 2 | Stapler | 4.50 |
| 1002 | P7 | 1 | Stapler | 4.50 |

`Quantity` genuinely depends on both — this order, this product. But `ProductName`
depends on ProductID alone. So the name is repeated on every order line containing
that product, and renaming the product means updating all of them.

Move it out to a Product table keyed on ProductID.

## Third normal form

In 2NF, and **no transitive dependencies** — no non-key attribute depends on
another non-key attribute.

| StudentID | Name | TutorID | TutorRoom |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| S1 | Turing | T3 | B12 |
| S2 | Hopper | T3 | B12 |

`TutorRoom` does not depend on StudentID except by going through TutorID:
StudentID $\rightarrow$ TutorID $\rightarrow$ TutorRoom. So the room is repeated
for every student that tutor teaches, and a room change means finding all of them.

Move TutorID and TutorRoom into a Tutor table.

<Aside kind="why" title="The one-line summary, and why it is genuinely sufficient">
> Every non-key attribute must depend on **the key, the whole key, and nothing but
> the key**.

"The key" is 1NF: there must *be* a key, which means atomic values and
distinguishable rows.
"The whole key" is 2NF: no depending on part of a composite key.
"Nothing but the key" is 3NF: no depending on some other non-key attribute.

This is not a memory aid bolted on afterwards; it is a complete statement of the
three forms. In an exam, writing it and then walking each attribute through it will
get you to the right answer more reliably than trying to recall the definitions
separately.
</Aside>

def determines(rows, left, right):
    """True if the values of the left attributes always fix the right one."""
    seen = {}
    for row in rows:
        key = tuple(row[a] for a in left)
        if key in seen and seen[key] != row[right]:
            return False
        seen[key] = row[right]
    return True

## When not to normalise

Normalisation removes redundancy, and redundancy is what makes reads fast.

A fully normalised database spreads one conceptual record across several tables, so
answering a question requires **joins**, and joins cost time. In a reporting system
running the same expensive query thousands of times an hour over data that barely
changes, that cost is paid over and over for a consistency risk that is close to
zero.

**Denormalisation** deliberately reintroduces redundancy for speed. It is a
legitimate engineering decision, and it is only legitimate when made deliberately:
the duplicated data must be regenerated by a controlled process, never edited in
two places by hand.

<Aside kind="trap" title="“Normalise as far as possible” is not the expected answer">
Questions about normalisation often invite a discussion, and the marks are for
recognising the trade-off rather than for reciting the benefits.

Normalise when the data changes often and correctness matters — a bank, a school
record system, anything transactional. Denormalise when the data is read far more
than written and speed is the constraint — reporting, analytics, caches.

An answer that only lists the advantages of 3NF has answered half the question.
</Aside>

<Takeaway>
Normalisation is one question asked of every attribute: what determines this? Put
it in the table whose key answers that, and the anomalies disappear on their own.
</Takeaway>
