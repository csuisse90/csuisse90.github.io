A photograph and a sound are both **continuous**: infinitely detailed, infinitely
divisible. A computer stores a **finite** list of numbers. Getting from one to the
other means throwing information away, deliberately and in a controlled fashion.
Everything in this topic is about how much to throw away and what it costs.

## Bitmap images

A **bitmap** divides the image into a grid of **pixels** and stores one colour per
pixel. Two numbers describe the arrangement.

**Resolution** is how many pixels there are, given as width $\times$ height. Note
that doubling both dimensions **quadruples** the pixel count — a mistake that
costs marks routinely.

**Colour depth** is how many bits store each pixel's colour. With $n$ bits you get
$2^n$ colours.

| Depth | Colours | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 bit | 2 | Pure black and white, fax |
| 8 bit | 256 | Old GIFs, indexed palettes |
| 24 bit | 16,777,216 | Standard photographs — 8 bits each of red, green, blue |
| 32 bit | as above, plus transparency | Images that composite over others |

</Figure>

The uncompressed size follows directly:

$$\text{size (bits)} = \text{width} \times \text{height} \times \text{depth}$$

A $1920 \times 1080$ photograph at 24-bit colour is $1920 \times 1080 \times 24 =
49{,}766{,}400$ bits, about $6.2\,\text{MB}$. A phone camera at 48 megapixels
produces about $144\,\text{MB}$ per shot uncompressed — which is why nothing is
ever stored uncompressed.

Files also carry **metadata**: the dimensions, the colour depth, often the date
and camera settings. This is not optional decoration. Without the width, a
decoder receiving three million colour values has no way to know whether they
form a wide image or a tall one.

<Aside kind="deeper" title="Why 24-bit is called “true colour” and roughly is">
The eye can distinguish somewhere around ten million colours, so $16.7$ million
was chosen as comfortably beyond the limit. It mostly is — but not entirely. In a
smooth gradient, such as a clear sky, adjacent bands differ by one step in one
channel, and the eye is unusually good at spotting edges. The result is visible
**banding**, which is why professional workflows use 10 or 12 bits per channel
and why HDR formats exist.

The general lesson is that "enough resolution" depends on what you are looking
at, not only on how much the eye can resolve in the abstract.
</Aside>

## Sound

Sound is a pressure wave: a continuously varying quantity. Digitising it means
**sampling** — measuring the amplitude at regular intervals and storing each
measurement.

<Figure caption="The smooth wave is the original. The bars are the samples. Everything between two bars is guessed on playback.">
  <Sampling />
</Figure>

**Sample rate** is how many measurements per second, in hertz. **Bit depth** is
how many bits store each measurement. The size follows:

$$\text{size (bits)} = \text{rate} \times \text{depth} \times \text{channels} \times \text{seconds}$$

CD audio is $44{,}100\,\text{Hz}$, 16 bits, 2 channels: $1{,}411{,}200$ bits per
second, or about $10\,\text{MB}$ a minute.

## Nyquist, and the number 44,100

Why $44.1\,\text{kHz}$? It looks arbitrary and is not.

The **Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem** states that to reconstruct a wave you
must sample at more than **twice** its highest frequency component. Below that,
the samples are consistent with more than one wave and there is no way to tell
which was recorded.

Human hearing tops out around $20\,\text{kHz}$, so the minimum is
$40\,\text{kHz}$. The extra $4.1\,\text{kHz}$ leaves room for the anti-aliasing
filter, which cannot cut off perfectly sharply at exactly $20\,\text{kHz}$.

<Aside kind="why" title="Aliasing is not just an audio phenomenon">
Sample too slowly and a high frequency becomes indistinguishable from a low one.
The recording then contains a tone that was never played.

You have seen this with your eyes. A wheel filmed at 24 frames per second appears
to slow, stop, then turn backwards as it speeds up: the camera is sampling the
wheel's rotation, and once the rotation exceeds half the frame rate, the samples
fit a slower backwards rotation just as well. Same theorem, same failure.

It is also why old computer graphics had jagged edges: an image is a spatial
sampling of a scene, and a diagonal line contains spatial frequencies higher than
the pixel grid can represent. "Anti-aliasing" in a game's settings is the same
filter, applied to space rather than time.
</Aside>

<Py caption="Sampling and quantisation, made visible. Run it to see the reconstruction error fall as bit depth rises, and watch aliasing appear when the rate drops below Nyquist.">{`
import math

def sample(freq, rate, depth, seconds=1.0):
    """Sample a sine wave and quantise it, returning the mean error."""
    levels = 2 ** depth
    total_error = 0.0
    n = int(rate * seconds)
    for i in range(n):
        t = i / rate
        true = math.sin(2 * math.pi * freq * t)
        step = round((true + 1) / 2 * (levels - 1))
        stored = step / (levels - 1) * 2 - 1
        total_error += abs(true - stored)
    return total_error / n

print("quantisation error against bit depth, 1 kHz tone")
for depth in (2, 4, 8, 12, 16):
    err = sample(1000, 44100, depth)
    print(f"  {depth:>2} bits  {2**depth:>6} levels  mean error {err:.6f}")

print()
print("file size for one minute of stereo audio")
for rate, depth in ((8000, 8), (22050, 16), (44100, 16), (96000, 24)):
    bits = rate * depth * 2 * 60
    print(f"  {rate:>6} Hz, {depth:>2}-bit -> {bits / 8 / 1_000_000:>6.2f} MB")
`}</Py>

## Quantisation error

Sampling fixes *when* you measure. **Quantisation** is what happens to the
measurement itself: the true amplitude is a real number, and it must be rounded
to one of the $2^n$ values the bit depth allows.

The difference is **quantisation error**, and it is heard as a faint noise
underlying the recording. Each extra bit halves the step size and so halves the
error — improving the signal-to-noise ratio by about $6\,\text{dB}$ per bit.
Sixteen bits puts the noise below the threshold of hearing for ordinary listening,
which is why it became the standard.

<Aside kind="trap" title="Sample rate and bit depth are not the same knob">
A common confusion: raising the sample rate does not reduce quantisation error,
and raising the bit depth does not capture higher frequencies.

- **Sample rate** governs the highest *frequency* you can record. Too low, and
  you get aliasing.
- **Bit depth** governs the *precision* of each measurement. Too low, and you get
  audible noise.

They are independent axes and questions often test whether you know which is
which. If the problem mentions treble, cymbals or frequency, it is sample rate.
If it mentions noise, dynamic range or quiet passages, it is bit depth.
</Aside>

<Takeaway>
Both images and sound are the same operation in different dimensions: chop a
continuous thing into regular pieces, and round each piece to the nearest number
you can afford to store. Resolution and sample rate control how finely you chop;
colour depth and bit depth control how finely you round.
</Takeaway>
