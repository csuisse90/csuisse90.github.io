Everything so far assumed numbers are positive. Since a register holds only zeros
and ones, and there is no bit that means "minus", the sign has to be encoded in the
bits themselves. How you choose to do that turns out to matter a great deal.

## The obvious method, and why it fails

Take the leftmost bit and call it the sign: $0$ for positive, $1$ for negative.
The rest holds the magnitude. So in eight bits, $+37$ is $0010\,0101$ and $-37$ is
$1010\,0101$.

This is **sign and magnitude**. It is easy for a human to read, and it has two
faults that turn out to be fatal.

**There are two zeros.** $0000\,0000$ is $+0$ and $1000\,0000$ is $-0$. They are
the same quantity with different bit patterns, so a comparison for equality has to
special-case them, and one of the 256 available patterns is wasted.

**Addition stops working.** Add $0010\,0101$ ($+37$) and $1010\,0101$ ($-37$) with
an ordinary adder and you get $1100\,1010$, which is $-74$ in this scheme. The
right answer is zero. So the hardware needs a separate subtractor, plus logic to
compare magnitudes and decide which operation to perform — a large amount of
silicon to handle a minus sign.

## Two's complement

The rule for negating a number is: **invert every bit, then add one.**

To find $-40$ in eight bits:

$$40 = 0010\,1000 \;\xrightarrow{\text{invert}}\; 1101\,0111 \;\xrightarrow{+1}\; 1101\,1000$$

That is it. Applying the same rule again gets you back: invert $1101\,1000$ to
$0010\,0111$, add one, and you have $0010\,1000$, which is $40$ once more. The
operation is its own inverse, which is a good sign that it is the natural one.

There is a shortcut worth learning, because it is faster and less error-prone
under exam pressure: **copy bits from the right up to and including the first
$1$, then invert everything to the left.** For $0010\,1000$, copy `1000`, then
invert `0010` to `1101`, giving `1101 1000`. Same answer, no carry to propagate.

## Reading one back

If the leading bit is $0$, read it as an ordinary binary number.

If the leading bit is $1$, the value is negative and there are two ways to find it.
Either apply invert-and-add-one and put a minus sign on the result, or — more
useful once it clicks — treat the leading column as **negative**. In eight bits the
place values become:

| $-128$ | 64 | 32 | 16 | 8 | 4 | 2 | 1 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 0 |

$-128 + 16 + 4 + 2 = -106$.

This second reading is not a trick; it is what two's complement *is*. The leading
bit is worth $-2^{n-1}$ and every other bit is worth what it always was.

<Aside kind="why" title="Why invert-and-add-one, of all things">
It looks arbitrary until you ask what number, added to $x$, gives zero in $n$ bits.

In eight bits, arithmetic happens modulo $256$: anything that carries past the
eighth bit is discarded, so $256 \equiv 0$. The negative of $x$ is therefore
whatever satisfies $x + y = 256$, that is $y = 256 - x$.

Now, $255 - x$ is exactly "invert every bit of $x$", because each bit of $x$ is
subtracted from a $1$. So $256 - x = (255 - x) + 1 = \text{invert}(x) + 1$.

Invert-and-add-one is not a rule someone invented. It is $256 - x$, computed
without a subtractor.
</Aside>

## Why hardware designers chose it

Because subtraction disappears.

$a - b$ is $a + (-b)$, and $-b$ is invert-and-add-one. So a processor needs only
an adder, a row of inverters, and a way to force a carry into the bottom bit. The
same circuit performs both operations, with one control line choosing between
them. Compared with building a separate subtractor and the logic to decide which
to use, this is an enormous saving — and it is why essentially every processor
built since the 1960s uses two's complement.

The single zero falls out for free: $-0$ would be invert($0000\,0000$) then
$+1 = 1111\,1111 + 1 = 0000\,0000$, discarding the carry. Zero negates to
itself.

## The lopsided range

Eight bits give 256 patterns. One is zero, 127 are positive, and that leaves 128
for negatives. So the range is $-128$ to $+127$: one more negative than positive.

$1000\,0000$ is $-128$, and it is the odd one out. Try to negate it — invert to
$0111\,1111$, add one, get $1000\,0000$ — and it negates to itself. There is no
$+128$ in eight bits for it to become. Every two's complement width has exactly
one value like this, and it is a genuine source of bugs in real software.

## Overflow

Add two numbers and the result may not fit. The rule for spotting it is precise:

> Overflow has occurred when two operands of the **same sign** produce a result of
> the **opposite sign**.

Adding a positive and a negative can never overflow, because the result is smaller
in magnitude than the larger operand.

<Py caption="Two's complement in eight bits, including the overflow rule. Step through the table and watch the sign bit.">{`
def to_twos(value, bits=8):
    return format(value & (2**bits - 1), f"0{bits}b")

def from_twos(pattern, bits=8):
    n = int(pattern, 2)
    return n - 2**bits if pattern[0] == "1" else n

for v in (40, -40, 127, -128, -1, 0):
    print(f"{v:>5} -> {to_twos(v)} -> back to {from_twos(to_twos(v))}")

## Shifts

Shifting the bits left one place multiplies by two; shifting right divides by two.
Both are far cheaper than a real multiply or divide, so compilers use them
whenever they can.

The subtlety is what to feed into the vacated position. On a **left** shift a zero
goes in at the right, and the danger is that a significant bit falls off the left,
silently changing the sign. On a **right** shift, an *arithmetic* shift copies the
sign bit into the top position so negatives stay negative, while a *logical* shift
inserts a zero — correct for unsigned values, wrong for signed ones.

<Aside kind="deeper" title="Arithmetic right shift does not round the way you expect">
$-7 \div 2$ shifted arithmetically gives $-4$, not $-3$. The shift discards the
bit that falls off the bottom, which rounds **towards negative infinity** rather
than towards zero. For positive numbers the two agree, so the discrepancy hides
until negatives appear.

This is why compilers cannot simply replace every division by two with a shift:
for signed values they must add a correction first. A small illustration of a
recurring theme — an optimisation that is obviously valid turns out to be valid
only over part of the range.
</Aside>

<Takeaway>
Two's complement is not a clever encoding of a minus sign. It is arithmetic
modulo $2^n$, in which "negative" simply means "the number that adds to zero" —
and that is why the ordinary adder keeps working.
</Takeaway>
