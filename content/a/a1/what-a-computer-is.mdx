Everything in this course — every gate, every network protocol, every sorting
algorithm — sits on top of one idea that took people a surprisingly long time to
have. It is worth getting completely straight before anything else, because
almost every confusion later on is really a confusion about this.

## Start with what a machine can do

Take away the screen, the keyboard and the word "computer" for a moment, and
think about a machine that does exactly one thing. A mechanical loom weaves
cloth. A music box plays a tune. Their behaviour is built into their shape: the
loom weaves *that* pattern because of how its parts are cut, and to weave a
different pattern you would have to take it apart and rebuild it.

That is a **special-purpose machine**. Its function and its physical form are the
same thing.

The Jacquard loom of 1804 did something quietly extraordinary. The pattern moved
off the machine and onto a stack of punched cards fed through it. The loom no
longer *was* a pattern; it was a machine that *read* patterns. Change the cards
and the same loom weaves something else, with no rebuilding at all.

<Takeaway>
A computer is what you get when you push that idea as far as it will go: a
machine whose behaviour is described by something you can change, rather than
built into something you cannot.
</Takeaway>

## The stored-program idea

The Jacquard loom kept its instructions outside itself, on cards. The decisive
step — set out in 1945 in a report on the EDVAC, and now usually called the
**von Neumann architecture** — was to put the instructions *inside* the machine,
in the very same memory that holds the data being worked on.

This sounds like a small change. It is not. It has two consequences that
between them create the entire subject.

**First, the machine is general-purpose.** The memory does not care what the
bits in it mean. Fill it with one program and you have a word processor; fill it
with another and you have a chess engine. One physical machine, unlimited
behaviours, all reachable without touching a screwdriver. Every app you have
ever installed is this idea being used.

**Second, a program is data.** Because instructions live in ordinary memory,
they can be read, copied, written and changed by other programs. This is not a
curiosity; it is load-bearing. A compiler is a program whose *output* is another
program. An operating system loads programs into memory as if they were files,
because to it, they are. Every one of those depends on the fact that the
distinction between instructions and data is a matter of interpretation, not a
matter of storage.

<Aside kind="history" title="Why 1945 and not 1845">
Charles Babbage designed a machine with essentially these properties a century
earlier and never finished building one. The obstacle was not the idea but the
engineering: the Analytical Engine was mechanical, and the precision needed for
thousands of interlocking brass parts was beyond what could be manufactured. The
idea had to wait for a technology — first relays, then valves, then transistors
— that could switch quickly and cheaply enough to make it practical.
</Aside>

## The machine does not understand anything

This is the part students most often get quietly wrong, and it costs marks in
questions that look like they are about something else.

When the processor meets an instruction, what actually happens is this: a
pattern of bits arrives at a circuit, and that pattern causes certain switches
to open and certain others to close. Electricity flows differently as a result.
That is the entire event. There is no step at which the machine considers what
the instruction is *for*.

An analogy that holds up well: a player piano rolling through a paper roll. Each
punched hole lets air through and a hammer strikes a string. The piano is not
performing Chopin — it is passing air through holes. The music exists in the
listener. In exactly the same way, meaning in a computer exists in the
programmer and the user, never in the machine.

<Aside kind="trap" title="A very common exam error">
Answers that say the computer "knows", "understands", "decides what to do" or
"realises" are describing something the hardware does not do, and examiners are
alert to it. Say what the machine does mechanically: it *fetches*, it *decodes*
a bit pattern, it *executes* a fixed operation, it *compares* two values and
*branches* on the result. Those are all things a switch can do. Understanding is
not.
</Aside>

## Why everything is two states

A modern processor is a very large collection of switches. A switch is either
conducting or not, so the natural unit of information is a choice between two
possibilities. That unit is a **bit**, written $0$ and $1$.

The obvious question is why stop at two. We have ten fingers and computers could
in principle use ten voltage levels, storing far more per wire.

The answer is noise. Inside a chip, a wire's voltage is never exactly what it
should be: nearby wires induce small currents, temperature shifts the behaviour
of every transistor, and no two transistors come off the production line
identical. If $0\,\text{V}$ means zero and $3.3\,\text{V}$ means one, then a
reading of $3.1\,\text{V}$ is unambiguous — it is nowhere near the middle, so it
is a one. The design has an enormous margin for error.

Now suppose ten levels, evenly spaced across the same range. Each is about
$0.37\,\text{V}$ from its neighbour, so a drift of a fifth of a volt turns a
seven into a six. At billions of operations per second across billions of
transistors, an error rate that small is still catastrophic.

<Aside kind="deeper" title="Where multi-level signalling is used anyway">
Two states is the right answer *inside a processor*, where components are cheap
and correctness is everything. It is not universally right. Flash memory
routinely stores three or four bits per cell by distinguishing eight or sixteen
charge levels, accepting a much higher error rate and correcting it with error-
correcting codes — worth it, because the alternative is more silicon. High-speed
network links do the same thing: gigabit Ethernet uses five voltage levels,
because there the expensive resource is the cable, not the electronics at each
end. The principle is that multi-level signalling is a trade of reliability for
density, and you make that trade where density is the scarce thing.
</Aside>

## The four-part model

With that in place, the standard description of any computer is almost
disappointingly simple. Four things happen:

| Stage | What it means | In a self-checkout |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Input | Getting data in from the outside world | Barcode scanner, touchscreen, card reader |
| Processing | Transforming that data according to instructions | Looking up prices, totalling, verifying payment |
| Storage | Holding data for later | The product database, the day's transaction log |
| Output | Presenting results to the outside world | Screen, receipt printer, the door unlocking |

Every topic in Theme A is a magnification of one of these boxes. The CPU is
processing. Data representation is what the bits in storage mean. Networks are
input and output stretched across a distance. Databases are storage taken
seriously.

## Hardware, software, and a blurred line

**Hardware** is the physical machinery. **Software** is the pattern of bits that
tells it what to do.

The distinction is real but softer than it sounds, and questions sometimes probe
that. Firmware — the code that starts a machine before an operating system
exists — is software, but it lives in the device and users never change it. Some
chips are field-programmable, meaning their *logic* is configured after
manufacture, so the wiring itself is software-defined. The useful way to hold
the distinction is by role: hardware is what stays when you power off, software
is what you can replace without a screwdriver.

<Aside kind="why" title="Why the course starts here">
It looks like a warm-up topic and it is not. Almost every hard question later on
— why a compiler and an interpreter differ, why an operating system is needed at
all, why a buffer overflow can execute an attacker's code — is answered by
"because instructions and data live in the same memory and the machine cannot
tell them apart". If that sentence is genuinely solid, later topics stop being a
list of facts to memorise.
</Aside>
